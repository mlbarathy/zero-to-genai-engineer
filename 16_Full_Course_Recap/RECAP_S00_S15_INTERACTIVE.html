S00 INTERVIEW

Session 00. The questions you will actually be asked.

Interview
What is an inverted index and why does it make search fast?
A data structure that maps each unique word to the list of documents containing it. Instead of scanning every document per query, you look up the word directly and get the document list in sub-millisecond time. A query like "python training" looks up both words, gets their document lists, then intersects them. This is how Elasticsearch, Lucene, and every major search engine indexes content.
Interview
What does TF-IDF measure and how does it rank better than simple word count?
TF (Term Frequency) measures how often a word appears in one document. IDF = log(total docs / docs containing word). Words in every document like "the" get IDF close to 0 and contribute nothing. Rare words get high IDF scores. TF x IDF is high for relevant, rare words and near zero for common filler words. This gives automatic stopword filtering built into the math, no explicit stopword list needed.
Interview
What is IDF when a word appears in every document? Why does this matter?
IDF = log(N/N) = log(1) = 0. The TF-IDF score becomes 0, so this word contributes nothing to ranking regardless of how often it appears. Same effect as a stopword list, without maintaining one: "the", "is", "a" appear in every document and score zero without needing an explicit stopword list. Conversely, a word appearing in 3 of 1000 documents gets IDF = log(333) ≈ 5.8, making it a very strong relevance signal.
S00 INTERVIEW

Limitation and BM25.

Interview
What is the core limitation of TF-IDF and how was it solved?
TF-IDF is purely lexical: it matches exact character strings. "car" and "automobile" have zero computed relationship. No understanding of synonyms, context, or meaning. This semantic gap was solved by word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe), which map words to dense vectors where semantically similar words land geometrically close. Modern systems use hybrid search: BM25 for exact keyword recall plus vector search for semantic understanding.
Interview
What is BM25 and why do most search engines use it over raw TF-IDF?
BM25 extends TF-IDF with two fixes. Term saturation: raw TF-IDF grows linearly with word frequency, so a word appearing 100 times scores 10x higher than appearing 10 times. BM25 adds a saturation parameter k1 (typically 1.5) so additional occurrences have diminishing returns. Document length normalization: parameter b penalizes long documents, because finding a rare word in a short document is more significant than in a 50,000-word one.
Interview
What are the main limitations of bag-of-words models?
Bag-of-words completely discards word order, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce identical vectors. It creates sparse, high-dimensional representations with vocabulary sizes of 100,000 or more. It treats all words as independent: "good" and "excellent" are as unrelated as "good" and "airplane." The word "bank" gets the same representation regardless of context. These limitations led directly to word embeddings (semantic gap) and then to Transformers (context-dependence gap).
Interview
Design a production search architecture. Where does the inverted index fit?
Ingestion pipeline: crawl documents, tokenize text, build the inverted index offline. At query time: tokenize the query, look up each token in the inverted index (O(1)), retrieve candidate lists, intersect them, score with BM25, return top-K results. For semantic search: add a vector index alongside and run approximate nearest-neighbor search. Use a reranker to improve precision on top results. This two-stage retrieve-then-rerank pattern is the same architecture used in production RAG systems.

S01 INTERVIEW

Session 01. Embeddings questions that separate candidates.

Interview
What is a word embedding and how is it different from one-hot encoding?
A word embedding maps each word to a dense vector (typically 100 to 300 dimensions) where semantic relationships are preserved as geometry. One-hot encoding maps each word to a sparse binary vector with a 1 at the vocabulary index and 0 everywhere else. Problems with one-hot: no semantic relationship between words, and a 100k vocabulary needs 100k-dimensional vectors. Word embeddings solve both: "king" and "queen" have high cosine similarity, and 300 dimensions represent the entire vocabulary meaningfully.
Interview
Why cosine similarity and not Euclidean distance for word vectors?
Word vectors have magnitudes that depend on word frequency during training. Common words end up with larger magnitudes. Euclidean distance is magnitude-sensitive: two vectors pointing in the same direction but with different lengths would score as different. Cosine similarity measures only the angle between vectors, ignoring magnitude. Same semantic meaning equals same direction equals high cosine similarity regardless of frequency. Range is -1 (opposite) to +1 (identical direction).
Interview
What is the critical limitation of Word2Vec and GloVe that Transformers solve?
Both Word2Vec and GloVe produce static embeddings: every word gets one fixed vector regardless of context. "Bank" gets the same vector whether it appears near "money" or "river." This polysemy problem is exactly what Transformers solve. Self-attention builds context-aware representations where the same word gets a different vector depending on its surrounding words. That is why BERT largely replaced Word2Vec for downstream NLP tasks.
Interview
What is FastText and when would you use it over Word2Vec?
FastText trains on character n-grams rather than whole words. "Playing" becomes ["pla", "lay", "ayi", "yin", "ing"]. This lets FastText generate a vector for words it never saw during training by composing character pieces. Use FastText when your data has typos or misspellings, you work with morphologically rich languages (German, Turkish), you have domain-specific technical vocabulary, or you work with code tokens. The cost is larger model size and slower inference.
Interview
How does Word2Vec learn embeddings without human labels?
Word2Vec uses self-supervised learning on a prediction task derived from text structure. Skip-gram: given a center word, predict surrounding context words. CBOW: given context words, predict the center word. Words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar weight vectors. After training on billions of sentences, the network weights converge to vectors that encode usage patterns, which turn out to encode semantic meaning.
Interview
What is the difference between Skip-gram and CBOW, and which handles rare words better?
Skip-gram: given center word, predict surrounding context words. Each center word generates multiple training examples, so the model focuses deeply on each individual word. CBOW: given context words, predict the center word. CBOW is 3 to 5 times faster but averages context signals. Skip-gram performs better for rare words because each step focuses on one word's context relationships. CBOW works better for frequent words and when training speed matters. Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (SGNS) is the industry standard for high-quality embeddings.
Interview
What is negative sampling and why was it needed?
The naive Word2Vec update computes a gradient over the entire vocabulary (100k words) for each training step: O(vocabulary size) operations per step. Negative sampling replaces the full softmax with a binary classifier: is this (center word, context word) pair real or noise? For each real pair, sample k noise words (5 to 20), and train to distinguish real from noise. Cost drops from O(vocabulary) to O(k) per update, a 5000 to 20000x speedup. The model still learns meaningful embeddings because distinguishing real context from random noise forces it to capture genuine co-occurrence patterns.

S02 INTERVIEW

Session 02. Attention questions, asked at every level.

Interview
Explain self-attention. Walk through Q, K, V.
Each token generates three learned linear projections: Query (what this token is searching for), Key (what this token offers as a label to others), and Value (the actual content this token contributes). Attention score = dot product of Q with each K, scaled by the square root of d_k to prevent softmax saturation, then softmax to get weights. Output = weighted sum of all V vectors using the attention weights. This runs in parallel for all tokens simultaneously, resolving contextual meaning in one pass.
Interview
Why does multi-head attention work better than single-head?
A single attention head must collapse all relationship types into one pattern. Multiple heads run in parallel, each with different learned weight matrices, so each head can simultaneously specialize. One head might track subject-verb dependencies, another pronoun-antecedent resolution, another semantic similarity. The outputs are concatenated and projected back down. Richer representations at the same computational cost as a single larger head.
Interview
Why divide by the square root of d_k in the attention formula?
As the embedding dimension d_k grows, dot products between Q and K grow in magnitude proportionally. Very large dot products push softmax into regions where one token gets probability close to 1 and all others close to 0. In this saturated regime, gradients drop to near zero and the model gets stuck. Dividing by the square root of d_k normalizes the scale, keeping softmax numerically stable with useful gradients.
Interview
Why does a Transformer need positional encoding?
Self-attention treats the input as a set with no inherent order. "The cat sat" and "sat cat The" would produce identical attention patterns without positional encoding. The original paper adds sinusoidal waves at different frequencies to each position. Modern models like Llama and Mistral use RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding), which rotates the Q and K vectors by position-dependent angles, encoding relative position directly into the attention computation and generalizing better to long sequences.
Interview
What is the difference between BERT and GPT architecturally?
BERT uses encoder-only Transformer layers with bidirectional self-attention: every token can attend to every other token in both directions. Pre-trained with Masked Language Modeling. Strong for classification, extraction, and sentence similarity. GPT uses decoder-only Transformer layers with causal self-attention: each token attends only to previous tokens. Pre-trained on next-token prediction. In the era of conversational AI, generation wins: GPT, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA, and Mistral are all decoder-only.
Interview
What does the Feed-Forward sublayer do in each Transformer block?
Each Transformer block has two sublayers. Multi-Head Self-Attention handles relationships between tokens. The Feed-Forward Network (FFN) is applied independently to each token. FFN(x) = max(0, xW1 + b1) x W2 + b2. The FFN is typically 4x wider than the embedding dimension. Some research traces factual knowledge (like "Paris is the capital of France") to specific FFN layer weights. Attention routes context across the sequence; FFN applies per-token transformations and retrieves memorized knowledge.

Interview
Why did DPO catch on so fast if RLHF already worked?
Cost and stability. RLHF needs three training runs, a separate reward model to maintain, and PPO, which is sensitive to hyperparameters and can collapse. DPO proves the optimal RLHF policy has a closed form, so you can skip the reward model and the RL loop entirely and train on preference pairs with one classification-style loss. Same alignment goal, far fewer moving parts, which matters when you are the one paying for the GPUs.
Interview
What is the KL penalty in RLHF protecting you from?
Reward hacking. The policy is being pushed to maximise a reward model that is only an approximation of human taste. Left unconstrained it will find degenerate text that scores highly and reads terribly. The KL term penalises drifting too far from the SFT model, so you buy alignment without losing fluency. If someone says "we removed the KL term to get higher reward", that is the bug, not the win.
S03 INTERVIEW

Session 03. Alignment questions people get wrong.

Interview
What is in-context learning and which model demonstrated it at scale?
In-context learning is the ability to perform new tasks from examples given in the prompt, with zero parameter updates. GPT-3 (2020, 175B parameters) demonstrated this at unprecedented scale. You give 2 to 3 input/output examples and the model generalizes to new inputs without any gradient updates. Before GPT-3, you fine-tuned for every new task. After GPT-3, a few examples in the prompt often suffice.
Interview
Walk through RLHF step by step. What is the KL penalty for?
RLHF has three stages. Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), start from a base model and fine-tune on human-written demonstrations of good responses. Stage 2: Reward Model Training, generate multiple responses to the same prompt, have humans rank them, train a separate model on these preference comparisons. Stage 3: RL with PPO, use Proximal Policy Optimization to update the SFT model to maximize reward model scores.
Interview
What is DPO and how does it differ from RLHF?
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) achieves the same alignment goal as RLHF but eliminates both the reward model and the RL training loop. RLHF requires three separate training stages: SFT, reward model, then PPO. This is expensive and unstable. DPO's insight: the mathematically optimal RLHF policy has a closed-form solution. You can convert the RL problem directly into a classification task on preference pairs.
Interview
What is hallucination and what are the main mitigations?
Hallucination is when a model confidently states something factually false: a fabricated citation, a wrong date, a person who does not exist. It happens because LLMs are trained to predict plausible next tokens, not to verify factual accuracy. There is no built-in "I don't know" signal.
Interview
What is Constitutional AI?
Constitutional AI (CAI, Anthropic 2022) addresses the scalability bottleneck of RLHF: human labelers must evaluate every output. CAI replaces human safety evaluators with the model itself, guided by a written "constitution" of principles. The model critiques its own response against these principles, generates a revised response, and these AI-generated preference pairs are used for training. Benefits: scales cheaply (principles applied to millions of examples automatically), and alignment criteria are explicit and auditable rather than implicit in human judgments. Claude's alignment training uses Constitutional AI as a core component.
Interview
What are emergent abilities in LLMs?
Emergent abilities appear suddenly at certain model scales, barely present in smaller models and then fully present in larger ones, without being explicitly trained for. A model at 7B parameters might score 5% on a task, and a 70B model suddenly scores 85%: not a gradual improvement but a phase transition. Examples: chain-of-thought reasoning, multi-step arithmetic, in-context learning. For engineers: you cannot predict capabilities by extrapolating from small models. A task that does not work at 7B may become trivial at 70B.
Interview
What were the key differences between GPT-1, GPT-2, and GPT-3?
GPT-1 (2018): 117M parameters, trained on roughly 1B tokens. Proved that unsupervised pre-training followed by fine-tuning works. Required fine-tuning for each downstream task. GPT-2 (2019): 1.5B parameters, trained on WebText (40GB of web text). Same architecture, dramatically bigger. Demonstrated zero-shot task completion: just a prompt, no fine-tuning. OpenAI initially withheld it over misuse concerns. GPT-3 (2020): 175B parameters, trained on 570GB of filtered internet text. Demonstrated in-context few-shot learning at scale: show 3 examples in the prompt and the model generalizes with zero gradient updates. One model could write code, translate, summarize, and answer questions from examples in the prompt alone. The pattern: scale (parameters plus data) was the primary lever, architecture barely changed.

S04 INTERVIEW

Session 04. Tokens, sampling, and the cost question.

Interview
Walk through the BPE algorithm step by step.
Start with a character-level vocabulary. Count all adjacent character pairs across the training corpus. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size (typically 50,000 to 100,000 tokens). Why not word-level: any word not in training becomes UNK, and every inflected form needs its own entry. Why not character-level: sequences become extremely long and attention computation (O(n squared)) becomes 16 to 25 times more expensive.
Interview
What is the correct order: temperature, softmax, Top-K, Top-P, sample?
Temperature always comes first because it reshapes the distribution before filtering. Correct order: (1) run Transformer forward pass to get raw logits, (2) divide logits by temperature, (3) apply softmax to convert to probabilities, (4) apply Top-K filter by zeroing out everything below rank K, (5) apply Top-P filter by keeping only the smallest set reaching cumulative probability P, (6) renormalize and sample.
Interview
What is the difference between Top-K and Top-P sampling?
Top-K always keeps exactly K candidates. Problem: if the model is very confident (one token at 95%), K=40 still forces sampling from 40 tokens including near-zero probability noise. If the model is uncertain (20 tokens at 5% each), K=40 might be too restrictive. Top-P keeps the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches P. When confident, Top-P might keep just 2 to 3 tokens. When uncertain, it adapts to keep more. Best practice: use both together (Top-K=40, Top-P=0.95).
Interview
GPT-4o charges $5 per 1M input tokens. A RAG system sends 3,000 tokens per query at 10,000 queries per day. What is the monthly input cost?
3,000 tokens x 10,000 queries = 30 million input tokens per day. At $5 per 1M tokens = $150 per day for input alone. Monthly: $150 x 30 = $4,500 in input costs. Add output (500 tokens x 10,000 queries = 5M output tokens per day, at $15 per 1M = $75 per day, $2,250 per month). Total: roughly $6,750 per month. Optimization: route simple queries to GPT-4o-mini (30 to 40 times cheaper), reduce context size by keeping top 3 retrieved chunks instead of 10, use prefix caching for repeated system prompts.
Interview
What is the "lost in the middle" problem in RAG?
Research shows LLMs systematically under-attend to information in the middle of long context windows. They perform best on information at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of the context, with accuracy dropping for content in the middle. In RAG: if you retrieve 10 documents and the most relevant one lands in the middle, it may effectively be ignored. Mitigations: put the most important retrieved context first or last, limit to top 3 to 5 chunks rather than 10 to 15, use a reranker to ensure the most relevant chunk gets a priority position.
Interview
A model generates "Eiffel Tower is in Berlin" confidently. Is this hallucination or retrieval failure?
This is a factual hallucination: the model generates false information with confidence. Retrieval failure means the correct document was not found. Hallucination means even with the correct document retrieved, the model generates something not supported by it. How to diagnose: check if the correct fact was in the retrieved context. If yes, it is hallucination (the model ignored the grounding evidence). If no, it is retrieval failure (fix chunking, reranking, or the embedding model). They require different fixes: retrieval failure needs better search; hallucination needs stricter grounding prompts and citation enforcement.

S05 INTERVIEW

Session 05. Running models, the practical questions.

Interview
What is GGUF format and what does Q4_K_M mean?
GGUF is the standard format for quantized LLM weights created by the llama.cpp project. All model weights, hyperparameters, tokenizer, and metadata are packed into one portable file with no separate config files needed. Q4_K_M breaks down as: Q4 = 4-bit quantization (each weight stored in 4 bits instead of 32), K = K-quant method (groups of roughly 32 weights share a scale factor to minimize quantization error), M = medium size variant (balances accuracy vs speed).
Interview
How does INT4 quantization reduce memory while preserving quality?
FP32 weights use 32 bits per parameter. INT4 uses 4 bits, an 8 times reduction. A 7B model goes from roughly 28GB (FP32) to roughly 3.5GB (INT4). The accuracy tradeoff: quantization rounds each weight to the nearest representable value in the 16-step INT4 range. For most weights, this rounding error is small because weight distributions are bell-shaped and concentrated near zero. K-quant methods use per-group scale factors to minimize this error further.
Interview
Compare Ollama, LM Studio, and OpenRouter: when would you choose each?
Ollama: CLI-first tool, runs a REST server at localhost:11434 with an OpenAI-compatible API. Best for developers, scripting, Jupyter notebooks, and production local inference. LM Studio: desktop GUI with drag-and-drop model loading, built-in chat interface, and a local OpenAI-compatible server. Best for non-developers and students exploring models visually. OpenRouter: cloud API gateway, no local install.
Interview
What are the three message roles in the universal chat format?
The universal format is a list of {role, content} dicts supported by every major provider. System: sets overall behavior, persona, and constraints for the entire conversation. User: represents a human turn. Assistant: represents a model turn. Including prior assistant turns is how you inject conversation history for multi-turn dialogue. Every provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Ollama) accepts this same format, which is why your conversation history logic works across providers without changes.
Interview
What are the limitations of OpenRouter for production use?
OpenRouter is a proxy, not a first-party provider. Limitations: (1) additional latency from the proxy hop (typically 50 to 200ms), (2) you depend on OpenRouter's uptime on top of the underlying provider's uptime, (3) pricing includes a small markup over direct API pricing, (4) some advanced features like fine-tuned model endpoints may not be available through the proxy, (5) enterprise SLAs come from OpenRouter, not the underlying providers. Use it for prototyping, development, and model comparison. For high-throughput production, connect directly to the provider.
Interview
How much RAM do you need to run a 7B model locally?
A 7B model at Q4_K_M requires roughly 4GB for weights plus 1 to 2GB for KV cache and overhead. Practical minimum: 6 to 8GB available RAM. Guide: 8GB RAM (MacBook Air M1/M2) runs 7B comfortably at 15 to 25 tokens per second using Apple Metal. 16GB handles 7B well or 13B at Q4_K_M. 32GB allows 13B comfortably or a try at 30B. 64GB handles 70B at Q4_K_M (roughly 40GB). Key rule: the model must fit entirely in RAM or VRAM for interactive speeds. Paging to disk is too slow for real use.
Interview
How does map-reduce work for documents that exceed the context window?
A single context window cannot fit a full book or large codebase. Map-reduce splits the document into chunks (map step), processes each chunk independently with the LLM, then combines results (reduce step). For summarization: map generates a summary of each chunk, reduce merges summaries into a final coherent output. For Q&A: map extracts relevant passages, reduce synthesizes the final answer. An alternative is the refine strategy: process chunk 1, pass its output plus chunk 2 to the LLM for refinement, repeat until done. Refine maintains cross-chunk coherence but is sequential and slower. Map-reduce is faster because chunks are processed in parallel. In LangChain: load_summarize_chain(chain_type="map_reduce").

S06 PROMPTS AS CODE

Handwritten prompts do not scale. So stop handwriting them.

This is the mental flip of the session. You stop tweaking strings and start declaring what you want, with a metric that scores it. The optimizer writes the prompt. Each rung below optimizes something the rung above could not touch.

LabeledFewShotDrops in k random training examples. No search, no scoring.
BootstrapFewShotRuns the model, keeps only the demos that passed your metric.
MIPROv2Searches the instruction wording as well as the examples.
GEPAReads its own failures and rewrites the prompt from them.
ready
Interview
What is DSPy and how does it differ from writing prompts manually?
DSPy (Stanford NLP) treats prompts as learnable parameters rather than hand-written strings. You define a Signature (input/output schema with field descriptions), a metric (function that scores output quality), and a training dataset. An optimizer automatically searches for the best prompt, instruction wording, and few-shot examples. The model never sees your raw prompt: DSPy assembles the optimal one from your signature and the optimized instructions.
Interview
What is the difference between LabeledFewShot and BootstrapFewShot?
LabeledFewShot randomly picks k examples from your training set and injects them as few-shot demonstrations. No actual optimization: just in-context learning with randomly selected examples. Quality depends on which k examples happen to be picked. BootstrapFewShot runs the model on training examples, evaluates each prediction against your metric, and keeps only examples where the model was correct. The injected demos are guaranteed to pass your validation criterion, not random samples.
Interview
What can MIPROv2 optimize that BootstrapFewShot cannot?
BootstrapFewShot selects which demonstrations to include but cannot modify the instruction text itself. MIPROv2 searches over both the instruction wording AND the few-shot examples simultaneously. It uses Bayesian optimization to generate and evaluate candidate instruction variants, focusing the search on promising phrasings. MIPROv2 may find "let's work through this step by step to ensure accuracy" outperforms your hand-written "answer the following question" by several points in F1.
Interview
What is Chain-of-Thought prompting and why does forcing reasoning help?
CoT prompting forces the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps before producing the final answer. Adding "think step by step" produces explicit reasoning tokens that give the model more context for each subsequent decision. Classic example: "Is 9.11 > 9.9?" Without CoT: "Yes" (wrong). With CoT: "9.11 means 0.11 after the decimal. 9.9 means 0.90. 0.11 < 0.90, so 9.11 < 9.9. No." (correct). CoT helps on multi-step math, logic puzzles, nuanced classification, and code generation.
Interview
What are the five parts of a well-written prompt and why does each matter?
Role (who the AI should be): activates relevant writing patterns from training. "You are a senior data analyst" produces very different output than the same task with no role. Task (what exactly to do): be specific, for example "summarize in 3 bullet points" not just "summarize." Context (background it needs): "the audience has no technical background" changes every word choice. Examples (2 to 3 input/output pairs): the most powerful lever for locking in format and label names, the model copies your exact output structure. Output format: critical when code must parse the output, use "Reply ONLY with valid JSON, no markdown, no explanation." Most bad prompts fail because only the task is included.
Interview
Give a concrete example where few-shot is strictly necessary over zero-shot.
Format consistency for downstream code parsing. Zero-shot "Classify sentiment as positive, negative, or mixed" produces "Positive", "positive", "POSITIVE", "The sentiment is positive", "I believe this is positive": all different strings, none reliably parseable by code. Few-shot with 3 examples ("Love it!" → "positive", "Awful!" → "negative") forces the model to lock into exactly one of three strings you control. Your if answer == "positive" check works 100% of the time. Rule: whenever downstream code must parse the output or you need consistent label strings for a classifier, few-shot is not optional. It is the only reliable approach.

One Runnable shape for every provider.

APIs are stateless. If the bot remembers, you resent history (or you added a checkpointer later).

Interview
What is LCEL and what does the pipe operator do technically?
LCEL (LangChain Expression Language) is a declarative way to compose LangChain components into pipelines using the | operator. Every component implements the Runnable interface. The | operator chains Runnables: output of the left component becomes input to the right. A chain like prompt | llm | StrOutputParser() means: (1) prompt formats the input dict into a messages list, (2) llm sends the messages to the provider and returns an AIMessage, (3) StrOutputParser extracts the .content string.
Interview
How does streaming work in LangChain and why is it critical for production?
chain.stream(input) returns a Python generator that yields text chunks as the LLM produces them, typically word by word. chain.invoke(input) waits for the complete response. Why streaming matters: users see the first word in under 1 second instead of waiting 5 to 20 seconds for a blank screen. Perceived speed is 5 to 10 times better even if total generation time is the same. Users can stop generation early if the response is going in the wrong direction. In Streamlit: st.write_stream(chain.stream(...
Interview
How would you switch a production chain from GPT-4 to Claude with minimal changes?
Because all LLM wrappers implement the same Runnable interface, you change exactly one line: replace ChatOpenAI with ChatAnthropic and update the model name. The chain prompt | llm | parser is unchanged. For production-grade switchability, define a factory function that reads from an environment variable: def get_llm(provider=None). Then switching in production means changing one environment variable, no code deployment needed.
Interview
What is ChatPromptTemplate and what is MessagesPlaceholder used for?
ChatPromptTemplate defines the message structure sent to the LLM as a template with typed variable slots. It takes a list of message tuples: [("system", "You are a {persona}"), ("human", "{question}")]. At runtime, .format_messages() fills in the variables and produces the final messages list. MessagesPlaceholder is a special slot for injecting a dynamic list of messages, specifically used for conversation history.
Interview
What is the Runnable interface and what are its 5 core methods?
The Runnable interface is the standard protocol every LangChain component implements, enabling composition with |. Five core methods: (1) .invoke(input): synchronous single call, use for simple scripts. (2) .stream(input): synchronous streaming, yields output chunks, use for real-time UIs. (3) .batch(inputs): processes a list of inputs efficiently, often in parallel, use for bulk processing. (4) .ainvoke(input): async version of invoke, use in FastAPI. (5) .astream(input): async streaming generator, use in async web servers. Every component that implements these methods works with |: prompts, LLMs, parsers, and custom RunnableLambda wrappers.
Interview
What is RunnableWithMessageHistory and how does it simplify multi-user chat?
Manual approach: maintain a message list, append each user and AI message, pass the full list to the chain on every turn. Works but requires boilerplate for every app, and multi-user isolation needs manual session keying. RunnableWithMessageHistory wraps any LCEL chain to automate all of this. You provide a get_session_history_fn that maps session_id to a history store. On each call, it retrieves history for the session, injects it into the MessagesPlaceholder, runs the chain, and appends the new turn automatically. Multi-user isolation: pass config={"configurable": {"session_id": user_id}}. Each user ID gets completely isolated history. Session persistence across restarts requires a Redis or database backend.

Interview
When do you use /goal versus /loop when working with a coding agent?
Use /goal when there is a finish line you can express as a command: build this until pytest passes, refactor until mypy is clean. The agent works autonomously and stops when the condition is true. Use /loop when the work has no finish line and should keep running, like a test watcher or a quality monitor, until you stop it yourself. One is a deliverable, the other is a daemon.
Interview
Why must the stop condition be a shell command rather than a description?
Because something has to decide when the loop ends, and it cannot be an opinion. A command either exits zero and prints what you expected or it does not. "The code looks good" cannot be evaluated, so the loop either never stops or stops on a guess. Write it as pytest exits 0 and prints "12 passed", or mypy prints "Success: no issues found". That is also what protects you from an agent that reports success on broken code.
Interview
Your agent edited three files and declared itself done. Tests were never run. What went wrong?
There was no verifiable stop condition, so "done" was the agent's own judgement. You gave it a task instead of a finish line. Rewrite it so the loop cannot end until a command you chose passes, and cap the turns so a failing loop does not run all night.
Interview
What goes into a /goal that does not go into an ordinary chat message?
Three things a chat message usually leaves out: the exact command that proves you are finished and its expected output, a turn limit, and the boundary of what must not change. Vague scope is an invitation for the agent to rewrite files you never asked it to touch.

S10 RERANK

Cheap search finds fifty. An expensive model picks the five.

A bi-encoder embeds the query and the document separately, so you can index millions of chunks in advance. Fast, but it never compares the two side by side. A cross-encoder reads the query and one chunk together and scores that pair. Far more accurate, far too slow to run on your whole corpus. So you use both, in stages.

Interview
Why not just use the cross-encoder for everything and skip retrieval?
Because it cannot be precomputed. A bi-encoder embeds each chunk once at ingestion, so query time is a vector lookup. A cross-encoder has to run the model on every query and chunk pair, so scoring a million chunks means a million forward passes per question. You would be waiting minutes and paying for all of it. Two stages give you the recall of cheap search and the precision of the expensive model, at the cost of only fifty extra forward passes.
Interview
Your recall at 50 is excellent but users still get bad answers. Where do you look?
Recall at 50 says the right chunk is somewhere in the pile, so retrieval is not the bug. Look at ordering and at how much you pass to the model. Either there is no reranker and the winner is buried mid-context, or you are stuffing all fifty chunks into the prompt and burying it in noise. Add the cross-encoder, cut what you send to the top three to five, and measure faithfulness before and after so you can prove the fix.
S10 INTERVIEW

Hireable RAG questions.

Interview
When does hybrid search beat dense-only retrieval?
When the query needs exact tokens dense models smear: error codes, SKUs, statute numbers, product IDs. BM25 catches those. Dense catches paraphrase. Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges both ranked lists so a doc that is strong on either list can win.
Interview
What does RAGAS faithfulness measure, and what failure does it catch?
Faithfulness asks whether claims in the answer are supported by the retrieved context. It catches fluent answers that drift or invent details not in the chunks, even when retrieval looked fine.
Interview
How is conversation memory different from RAG?
RAG retrieves documents for this question. Memory keeps the dialogue state: thread_id, checkpointer, trimming, summarization, long-term Store. A production chatbot needs both. Memory without RAG still hallucinates policy. RAG without memory forgets the prior turn.
Interview
Your bot invents a 30-day refund. Retrieval returned nothing useful. Is that hallucination, retrieval failure, or both?
Primary failure is retrieval (or empty context). The model then fills the gap with a fluent guess, which is hallucination. Fix retrieval and grounding first. Add refusal when context is weak.
Interview
You retrieved 10 chunks. The right fact sits in chunk 6. What risk is that?
Lost-in-the-middle. Models attend better to start and end of long context. Put the best chunks first (or last), or rerank so the winner is not buried.
Interview
Faithfulness is low but answer relevance looks fine. What failed?
The model wrote a fluent answer that is not supported by the retrieved context. That is the classic RAG hallucination signal RAGAS is meant to catch.

Interview
When do you use LangGraph instead of a simple scripted chain?
When you need branching, retries, cycles, durable state, or human pause and resume. A single prompt with no tools does not need a graph. Graphs earn their keep when control flow is the product.
Interview
How does interrupt() support human-in-the-loop, and why does thread_id matter?
interrupt() pauses the graph before a risky side effect. The human answers yes or no. The same thread_id resumes from checkpointed state. A new thread_id is a new conversation and loses the pause context.
Interview
What is a supervisor pattern in multi-agent systems?
A routing agent delegates to specialists (RAG, SQL, tickets) instead of one agent doing everything. The supervisor chooses who acts next and when to finish. That is how the helpdesk orchestrator splits knowledge work from ops writes.
Interview
When is a supervisor worth the complexity over one ReAct agent?
When specialists need different tools and prompts, and a router must pick who acts. One agent with 20 tools gets confused. Supervisor routes, workers stay narrow.
Interview
What breaks if you resume HITL on a brand new thread_id?
You lose the checkpoint. interrupt() only resumes on the same thread. New thread means a new run with no paused state.
S11 HUMAN IN THE LOOP

The graph stops, waits for a human, and carries on where it left off.

This is the feature that makes agents allowed near production. Before a risky side effect, the graph pauses. Its state is checkpointed under a thread_id. A human approves or rejects. The same thread resumes from exactly that point. Press Play to walk it.

ready
Interview
A reviewer asks why you did not just use an if statement and a confirm dialog. Answer them.
A confirm dialog only works if the process is still alive and still holding the half-finished work in memory. Real approvals take minutes or days, the server restarts, the user closes the tab. Checkpointing the graph state against a thread_id means the run survives all of that and resumes at the exact node it paused on, with the tool call it wanted to make still intact. It also gives you an audit trail of what was proposed and who approved it, which is usually what the reviewer actually wants.

S15 MEMORY MATH

Do the memory arithmetic. It decides the whole design.

Updating every weight in a 7B model needs roughly 112 GB of GPU memory: two bytes for the weight, two for the gradient and about twelve for optimizer state, times seven billion. A free Colab GPU has about 15 GB. Full fine-tuning is the right idea on the wrong budget, and everything else in this session follows from that one gap.

112 GBfull fine-tune of 7B, about 16 bytes per parameter
14 GBbase in FP16, the LoRA case
3.5 GBbase in NF4, the QLoRA case
3.55 GBQLoRA total: NF4 base plus FP16 adapters
Read the last two boxes together. Quantizing the base from FP16 to NF4 is what drags 14 GB down to 3.5 GB, and the adapters on top cost about 0.05 GB. That is how a 7B fine-tune fits on a free GPU.
S15 LORA

Freeze the big matrix. Train two thin ones.

The adapters are the cheap part. For a 512 by 512 layer at rank 8 you store 8,192 numbers instead of 262,144, roughly three percent. The formula worth memorising: trainable is about 2 times d times r, not d squared. Bigger layers make the saving better, not worse.

ready
Quantization is compression, not teaching. Squeezing the base to four bits is what makes training fit on one GPU. It does not put a single new fact into the model.
S15 INTERVIEW

Session 15. RAG or fine-tune, and what quantization is not.

Interview
When do you choose RAG versus fine-tuning?
RAG for facts that change and need citations (policy, prices). Fine-tuning for stable behavior: tone, format, slang. Many products use both: fine-tune the voice, RAG the facts.
Interview
What does quantization change, and what does it not change?
Quantization stores weights with fewer bits so the model fits in memory (for example FP16 to INT4). It does not teach new documents. QLoRA quantizes the base, then trains LoRA adapters on top.
Interview
In one sentence, what does LoRA change during training?
It freezes the base weights W0 and learns small low-rank matrices B and A so the update is roughly B times A, which is cheap to train and easy to merge or swap.
Interview
Customer asks for yesterday's FX rate. You only have LoRA adapters. Enough?
No. Rates change. That is RAG (or a live tool). LoRA changes how the bot talks, not yesterday's number.
Interview
Q4 quantization made the model fit on your laptop. Did it learn your PDF?
No. Quantization only stores weights with fewer bits. The PDF still needs RAG or fine-tune data. Bits are not knowledge.

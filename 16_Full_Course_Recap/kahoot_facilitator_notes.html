Q1S00

A word appears in every document in the corpus. What is its IDF?

Answer: Zero, so it contributes nothing to ranking

Why: IDF is log(N/df). When df equals N that is log(1) which is exactly 0, so the term drops out of the score entirely.

Trap: People guess 'maximum' because the word is everywhere. Frequency across documents is what KILLS the signal, not what creates it. This is a free stopword list built into the maths.

Q2S00

What actually makes an inverted index fast at query time?

Answer: Cost tracks the matches, not the corpus size

Why: The index is built once at ingestion. At query time you jump straight to the posting list for that word, so work scales with the number of matches.

Trap: 'Scans in parallel' is the trap: parallel scanning is still reading the whole corpus. The win is not reading it at all.

Q3S00

Why does TF-IDF score zero for 'car crash' when you search 'automobile accident'?

Answer: It matches character strings, not meaning

Why: TF-IDF is purely lexical. It compares character strings, and these two phrases share none, so every term contributes zero.

Trap: Nothing is wrong with the documents or the stemmer. The method itself has no concept of meaning. That gap is the entire reason embeddings exist.

Q4S00

BM25 adds term saturation on top of TF-IDF. What does that fix?

Answer: The 50th hit should not count like the 1st

Why: Raw TF grows linearly, so a word appearing 50 times scores 50 times higher. BM25's k1 makes extra occurrences give diminishing returns.

Trap: Length normalisation is real but that is the b parameter, not saturation. Keep the two straight: k1 saturates frequency, b handles length.

Q5S00

What does the b parameter in BM25 control?

Answer: How strongly document length is normalised

Why: b sets how hard long documents are penalised. Finding a rare word in a short document means more than finding it in a 50,000 word one.

Trap: Saturation of term frequency is k1, not b. Mixing up the two is the single most common BM25 slip in interviews.

Q6S01

Why prefer cosine similarity over Euclidean distance for text embeddings?

Answer: It compares direction and ignores length

Why: Vector length tracks things like word frequency and document length. Cosine measures only the angle, so meaning survives a long and a short text saying the same thing.

Trap: Euclidean works fine in high dimensions mathematically. The problem is not that it breaks, it is that it punishes length differences you do not care about.

Q7S01

Word2Vec gives 'bank' a single vector. What does that cost you?

Answer: River bank and money bank share one vector

Why: Word2Vec embeddings are static: one vector per word for life, regardless of the sentence around it. Polysemy is unrepresentable.

Trap: Nothing about vocabulary size or labels is the issue. This specific limitation is what self-attention was built to solve.

Q8S01

What problem does negative sampling solve when training Word2Vec?

Answer: Full-vocabulary softmax is too expensive

Why: The naive update needs a softmax over the whole vocabulary on every step. Negative sampling replaces it with a binary real-or-noise choice over a handful of samples.

Trap: It is a compute optimisation, not a quality fix for rare words. The embeddings stay meaningful because telling real context from noise still forces real co-occurrence learning.

Q9S01

How can FastText embed a word it never saw during training?

Answer: It composes it from character n-grams

Why: FastText trains on character n-grams, so an unseen word is assembled from sub-word pieces it does know. This is why it survives typos and rich morphology.

Trap: There is no dictionary lookup and no random fallback. If you said 'copies the nearest word' you are describing a different system entirely.

Q10S01

Skip-gram or CBOW for rare words, and why?

Answer: Skip-gram, each centre word drives more updates

Why: Skip-gram predicts context from one centre word, so each occurrence of a rare word generates several focused training examples.

Trap: CBOW averages the surrounding context, which drowns out the rare word's own signal. CBOW is faster and better on frequent words, which is the opposite trade.

Q11S02

In self-attention, what does the Key vector represent?

Answer: What a token advertises to be matched against

Why: Key is the label a token advertises so other tokens can match against it. Query is what a token is looking for, Value is what gets passed on.

Trap: Mixing up Query and Key is the classic slip. Say it as a room of people: Query is your question, Key is everyone's name badge, Value is what they actually tell you.

Q12S02

Why are attention scores divided by the square root of d_k?

Answer: Big dot products saturate softmax, killing gradients

Why: Dot products grow with dimension. Large values push softmax into a near one-hot regime where gradients vanish and learning stalls.

Trap: This has nothing to do with positions or parameter count. It is purely about keeping softmax in a range where it still has useful gradients.

Q13S02

What does multi-head attention buy over one single larger head?

Answer: Heads specialise in different relations in parallel

Why: Each head has its own projections, so heads can specialise in different relationships at once, such as syntax in one and coreference in another.

Trap: It does not replace the feed-forward layer or change the context window. One big head has to collapse every relationship type into a single pattern.

Q14S02

With no positional encoding, how does a Transformer see its input?

Answer: As an unordered set, so word order is lost

Why: Attention is permutation invariant. Without position information 'the cat sat' and 'sat cat the' produce identical representations.

Trap: The model still runs, which is what makes this subtle. It runs and silently loses all word order.

Q15S02

What does RoPE encode that absolute sinusoidal encodings do not?

Answer: Relative position, by rotating Q and K

Why: RoPE rotates Q and K by a position-dependent angle, so the attention score depends on the distance between two tokens rather than their absolute slots.

Trap: Absolute sinusoidal encodings give each position its own fixed signal. Relative position is what generalises better to longer sequences.

Q16S02

Architecturally, how do BERT and GPT differ?

Answer: BERT attends both ways, GPT only to the past

Why: BERT is encoder-only with bidirectional attention, trained with masked language modelling. GPT is decoder-only with causal attention, trained on next-token prediction.

Trap: If you reversed them, remember it this way: GPT generates, so it must not see the future. BERT classifies, so it may see everything.

Q17S02

A Transformer block has attention and a feed-forward net. What does the FFN do?

Answer: Transforms each token on its own, after mixing

Why: Attention moves information between tokens. The FFN then transforms each token position independently, and research traces a lot of stored factual knowledge to those weights.

Trap: If you said it mixes tokens, you described attention. Keep the division clean: attention routes across the sequence, FFN transforms per token.

Q18S03

What did GPT-3 demonstrate that earlier GPT models did not?

Answer: Learning a task from prompt examples alone

Why: Few-shot in-context learning at scale. Put two or three examples in the prompt and the model generalises with zero weight updates.

Trap: GPT-2 already showed zero-shot behaviour, so 'learning from the prompt' is the distinguishing jump, not the invention of attention or tokenizers.

Q19S03

What is the correct order of the three RLHF stages?

Answer: SFT, then reward model, then PPO

Why: Supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations, then a reward model trained on human rankings, then PPO optimising against that reward.

Trap: Every wrong option puts PPO before the thing it optimises against. You cannot run RL before you have a reward signal to maximise.

Q20S03

What is the KL penalty in RLHF guarding against?

Answer: Drifting off SFT until output quality collapses

Why: It keeps the policy close to the SFT model. Without it the policy finds degenerate text that games the reward model while reading terribly.

Trap: This is reward hacking, not a memory or tokenizer issue. If a team says they removed the KL term to get higher reward, that is the bug, not the win.

Q21S03

Compared with RLHF, what does DPO remove?

Answer: The reward model and the RL loop

Why: DPO shows the optimal RLHF policy has a closed form, so you can train directly on preference pairs and delete both the reward model and the RL loop.

Trap: It still needs preference data and still starts from a fine-tuned model. What goes away is the machinery in the middle, not the data.

Q22S03

In Constitutional AI, where does most safety feedback come from?

Answer: The model critiquing itself against principles

Why: The model critiques its own output against a written constitution and revises it. Those AI-generated preferences then drive training.

Trap: The whole point is removing the human from the per-example loop, because human labelling is the bottleneck. Humans write the principles, not the labels.

Q23S03

Labellers preferred 1.3B InstructGPT over 175B GPT-3. What does that show?

Answer: Alignment can beat raw scale on usefulness

Why: Alignment and raw scale are different levers. A much smaller aligned model was preferred around 85% of the time.

Trap: This does not mean scale is irrelevant or that small is always better. It means being useful is not the same property as being capable.

Q24S03

What are emergent abilities in large language models?

Answer: Skills that appear suddenly past a certain scale

Why: Capabilities almost absent at small scale that appear sharply past a size threshold, like multi-step arithmetic or chain-of-thought reasoning.

Trap: They are not programmed in or toggled on. The engineering consequence is that you cannot extrapolate what a 70B model will do from testing a 7B one.

Q25S03

Why do language models hallucinate so confidently?

Answer: They optimise plausible next tokens, not truth

Why: They are trained to predict the most plausible next token, and nothing in that objective rewards being right or signals 'I do not know'.

Trap: Bad training data and high temperature make it worse but are not the root cause. A perfectly trained model at temperature zero still has no truth check, which is why RAG and citations exist.

Q26S04

What is the correct decoding order?

Answer: Temperature, top-k, top-p, then sample once

Why: Temperature reshapes the distribution first, then top-k and top-p cut the tail, then you draw a single sample.

Trap: Sampling before filtering means you already picked a token, so the filters do nothing. This is a real production bug, not a theoretical one.

Q27S04

Why does BPE beat a pure word-level vocabulary?

Answer: Unseen words split into known sub-words

Why: Any unseen word decomposes into sub-words that are in the vocabulary, so there is no out-of-vocabulary token.

Trap: Word-level actually produces SHORTER sequences, so that option is backwards. The trade you accept with BPE is more tokens for full coverage.

Q28S04

Top-k is 40 and the model is very confident. What is the risk?

Answer: You still sample from 39 near-zero options

Why: Top-k keeps a fixed count regardless of confidence. If one token holds most of the mass, you are still drawing from 39 near-zero options.

Trap: It does not force the top token, which is exactly the danger. Fixed k ignores how sure the model actually is.

Q29S04

What does top-p sampling do that top-k cannot?

Answer: Varies how many tokens survive with confidence

Why: Top-p keeps the smallest set reaching cumulative probability p, so the candidate count shrinks when the model is confident and grows when it is unsure.

Trap: It is not deterministic, that would be greedy decoding at temperature zero. Top-p adapts the cut, it does not remove randomness.

Q30S04

Why does a long prompt suffer from 'lost in the middle'?

Answer: Models attend best to the start and the end

Why: Models attend most reliably to the beginning and end of a long context. Content in the middle can be effectively ignored even though it was retrieved.

Trap: Nothing is truncated or compressed. The text is present and the model simply under-attends to it, which is why reranking matters.

Q31S04

3,000 tokens a query, 10,000 queries a day, $5 per 1M input tokens. Monthly input cost?

Answer: About $4,500

Why: 3,000 x 10,000 = 30M tokens a day. At $5 per million that is $150 a day, so about $4,500 a month for input alone.

Trap: The common slip is dropping a zero. Do it in two steps: tokens per day first, then dollars per day, then times thirty.

Q32S05

A 7B model is about 28GB in FP32. Roughly what does 4-bit quantization give?

Answer: About 3.5GB

Why: FP32 is 4 bytes a weight, 4-bit is half a byte, so you divide by eight. 28GB becomes about 3.5GB.

Trap: 14GB is FP16 and 7GB is INT8. Those are all real numbers for different precisions, which is what makes this worth getting right.

Q33S05

In the tag Q4_K_M, what does Q4 mean?

Answer: Four bits are used per weight

Why: Four bits per weight. K is the K-quant grouping method and M is the medium size variant balancing accuracy against speed.

Trap: Nothing in the tag refers to GPUs, context length or version number. Each letter is about how weights are stored.

Q34S05

What is the real engineering argument for running a model locally?

Answer: Data stays put and tokens cost nothing

Why: Data never leaves the machine, it works offline, and there is no per-token bill. You pay in RAM and accept a quality gap.

Trap: Local does not beat frontier quality, and claiming it does in an interview will cost you. The honest answer names the trade.

Q35S05

What is the practical test of whether a local model is usable?

Answer: It must fit in RAM or VRAM, or it crawls

Why: The weights must fit entirely in RAM or VRAM. Once it pages to disk, speed collapses below anything interactive.

Trap: Parameter count alone does not decide it, because quantization changes the footprint. Fit is the rule, not size.

Q36S05

A document dwarfs the context window. What does map-reduce do?

Answer: Summarise each chunk, then combine the summaries

Why: Map: process each chunk independently and summarise it. Reduce: combine those summaries into the final answer. Chunks can run in parallel.

Trap: Truncating loses content and context windows are fixed at inference. The alternative is refine, which is sequential and slower but keeps cross-chunk coherence.

Q37S05

In the universal chat format, what is the system role for?

Answer: Behaviour and limits for the whole conversation

Why: System sets persona, behaviour and constraints for the whole conversation. User is a human turn, assistant is a model turn.

Trap: It is not metadata and the model certainly does not ignore it. Replaying prior assistant turns is how you give a stateless API memory.

Q38S06

When is few-shot strictly better than zero-shot?

Answer: When code must parse an exact output shape

Why: When code downstream must parse the output. Zero-shot returns 'Positive', 'positive', 'POSITIVE' and sentences; examples lock one exact string.

Trap: Few-shot costs MORE tokens, so cost is never the reason. You pay those tokens to buy format reliability.

Q39S06

Why does chain-of-thought help on multi-step problems?

Answer: Intermediate tokens give later steps context

Why: Generating intermediate steps gives the model more relevant context for each subsequent decision instead of jumping straight to an answer.

Trap: It does not change weights or disable sampling. It changes what is in the context when the final token is chosen.

Q40S06

In DSPy, how does BootstrapFewShot differ from LabeledFewShot?

Answer: It keeps only demos that passed your metric

Why: Bootstrap runs the model on training examples, scores them with your metric, and keeps only the ones that passed.

Trap: LabeledFewShot is the one that picks at random. Neither touches instruction wording, which is what MIPROv2 adds.

Q41S06

What can MIPROv2 optimise that BootstrapFewShot cannot?

Answer: The instruction wording itself

Why: MIPROv2 searches over instruction phrasing as well as examples, using Bayesian optimisation to explore candidate wordings.

Trap: No DSPy optimizer touches weights, the tokenizer or embedding size. They all operate on the prompt.

Q42S06

What makes GEPA different from the earlier DSPy optimizers?

Answer: It reflects on its own failures and rewrites

Why: GEPA reflects on its own failed outputs and rewrites the prompt from what went wrong, rather than only selecting among examples.

Trap: Bootstrap selects demos and MIPROv2 searches instruction wording. None of them, GEPA included, touch the model's weights.

Q43S07

Your bot forgot the user's name from two turns ago. Root cause?

Answer: The API is stateless, history was not resent

Why: Chat APIs are stateless. The model sees only what you send on this call, so prior turns must be resent every time.

Trap: Nothing was deleted by the provider. If your bot remembers, it is because you stored and resent history, or added a checkpointer.

Q44S07

What does the pipe operator in LCEL actually do?

Answer: Feeds one Runnable's output into the next

Why: It composes Runnables: the output of the left becomes the input of the right. Prompt formats, model generates, parser extracts.

Trap: It is sequential composition, not concurrency and not caching. Everything in the chain implements one shared interface.

Q45S07

Total generation time is unchanged. Why stream anyway?

Answer: First token lands fast, so it feels quicker

Why: Time to first token drops to under a second, so perceived speed improves several-fold even with identical total time. Users can also stop early.

Trap: You are billed for the same tokens either way. The win is entirely in perception and control.

Q46S07

You swap ChatOpenAI for ChatAnthropic. Why does the rest of the chain survive?

Answer: Both implement the same Runnable interface

Why: Every chat model wrapper implements the same Runnable interface, so the prompt and parser either side are unaffected. It is a one-line change.

Trap: LangChain does not rewrite your prompt or translate key formats. The abstraction is the shared interface, nothing more.

Q47S07

What is MessagesPlaceholder for in a ChatPromptTemplate?

Answer: A slot where conversation history is injected

Why: It reserves a slot in the template where a list of prior messages gets injected at runtime, which is how multi-turn history enters the prompt.

Trap: It is not a fallback or a cache. Without it you would have to rebuild the whole message list by hand on every single turn.

Q48S09

What counts as a valid stop condition for an autonomous coding goal?

Answer: A command with exact expected output

Why: Something a machine can evaluate: a shell command plus its exact expected output, such as pytest exiting 0 and printing '12 passed'.

Trap: 'The code looks good' cannot be evaluated, so the loop either never ends or ends on a guess. That is how agents report success on broken code.

Q49S09

When do you want a repeating loop rather than a one-shot goal?

Answer: When work recurs and has no finish line

Why: When the work recurs and has no finish line, like a test watcher or a quality monitor. A goal ends, a loop runs until you stop it.

Trap: Task size is irrelevant. A huge task with a clear finish line is still a goal.

Q50S09

Your agent edited three files and declared success. No tests ran. What failed?

Answer: There was no verifiable stop condition

Why: There was no verifiable stop condition, so 'done' was the agent's own judgement rather than a check you defined.

Trap: Model size is not the issue. Give it a command that must pass and a turn cap, and this cannot happen.

Q51S10

Your chunks are too small. What typically breaks?

Answer: One answer splits across chunks and is lost

Why: A single answer gets split across chunk boundaries, so no individual chunk carries the whole fact and retrieval returns fragments.

Trap: Nothing technical fails. Embeddings and BM25 work normally, which is why this is hard to spot without evaluating.

Q52S10

Why add overlap between adjacent chunks?

Answer: A sentence cut by a boundary still survives

Why: Overlap means a sentence cut by a boundary still appears intact in one of the two chunks.

Trap: It increases storage rather than reducing it. You are deliberately paying duplication to protect boundary content.

Q53S10

A user searches the code ERR-4042. Why does dense-only retrieval miss it?

Answer: Embeddings blur rare exact strings

Why: Embeddings capture semantics. Rare exact strings like error codes and SKUs have little semantic signal, so they get blurred against similar-looking codes.

Trap: Dense retrieval does not ignore numbers wholesale. It just has no reason to treat this exact string as distinctive, which is where BM25 wins.

Q54S10

What does Reciprocal Rank Fusion actually combine?

Answer: Rank positions from each list, not raw scores

Why: RRF sums 1/(k + rank) across lists. It uses rank position only, so you never have to make BM25 scores and cosine scores comparable.

Trap: Combining raw scores is exactly what RRF avoids, because those scores are on incompatible scales.

Q55S10

Why can a cross-encoder not replace your first-stage retriever?

Answer: Nothing can be precomputed, so it scores every pair

Why: A bi-encoder embeds chunks once at ingestion. A cross-encoder must run the model on every query-document pair, so a million chunks means a million forward passes per question.

Trap: It is MORE accurate, not less. Accuracy is why you use it on the top 50, and cost is why you cannot use it on everything.

Q56S10

You pass 10 chunks and the right one sits 6th. What is the risk?

Answer: It lands mid-context where attention is weakest

Why: Mid-context content gets the least reliable attention, so retrieval technically succeeded while the answer still comes out wrong.

Trap: Nothing discards it. The chunk is right there in the prompt and under-used, which is why reranking into the top few matters.

Q57S10

RAGAS faithfulness is low but answer relevance is high. What happened?

Answer: It sounds on-topic but the context does not support it

Why: The answer is on-topic but makes claims the retrieved context does not support. That is the textbook signature of RAG hallucination.

Trap: High relevance tells you retrieval and topic matching worked. The failure is in generation drifting beyond its evidence.

Q58S10

How does conversation memory differ from RAG?

Answer: Memory holds dialogue state, RAG fetches documents

Why: Memory carries dialogue state across turns via a thread and checkpointer. RAG fetches documents for the current question. Production bots need both.

Trap: They are not interchangeable. Memory without RAG still invents policy; RAG without memory forgets what you said one turn ago.

Q59S10

A retrieved document contains 'ignore your previous instructions'. What is this?

Answer: Indirect prompt injection via the knowledge base

Why: Indirect prompt injection. The attack arrives inside retrieved content rather than from the user, which is why input filtering alone misses it.

Trap: This is not a chunking or encoding artefact. Treat retrieved text as untrusted data, never as instructions.

Q60S10

Recall at 50 is excellent but answers are still poor. Where do you look first?

Answer: Ordering and how much context you pass

Why: Good recall means the right chunk is in the candidate pile, so retrieval is not the bug. Look at ordering and how much you stuff into the prompt.

Trap: Swapping the embedding model or vector database changes retrieval, which you just proved is working. Fix ranking and context size instead.

Q61S10

What does a recursive character splitter do that fixed-size splitting does not?

Answer: Break on paragraph and sentence boundaries first

Why: It tries natural boundaries in order, paragraph then sentence then word, and only falls back to a hard cut. Chunks end up uneven but semantically whole.

Trap: Equal-size chunks are exactly what fixed-size splitting gives you, and exactly what cuts sentences in half. Uneven is the feature here.

Q62S10

You need a vector store running locally in a notebook with no setup. Which fits?

Answer: FAISS or Chroma, both run in-process

Why: FAISS and Chroma run in-process with no server, which is why they work in Colab. Chroma also persists to disk easily.

Trap: Pinecone is managed and needs an API key and network. A plain SQL database has no vector index or similarity search without an extension.

Q63S10

SPLADE is called a learned sparse retriever. What does that mean?

Answer: It learns sparse term weights with a model

Why: SPLADE predicts a sparse weight over vocabulary terms, including terms not literally in the text, so you get BM25-style exact matching plus some vocabulary expansion.

Trap: It is not a small dense encoder. Sparse here describes the vector, most entries being zero, not how the files are stored.

Q64S10

RAGAS and DeepEval both score a RAG system. What do they share?

Answer: Both use an LLM as judge against your context

Why: Both use an LLM as a judge, comparing the answer against the retrieved context to produce metrics like faithfulness without hand-labelled ground truth.

Trap: That is the appeal and the caveat: no human labels needed, but your scores inherit the judge model's own biases.

Q65S10

Your PDF has charts the text layer never describes. What does multimodal RAG add?

Answer: A vision model describes images so they index

Why: A vision model looks at the image or the rendered page and writes a description, which gets embedded and indexed alongside the text.

Trap: Larger chunks cannot help, because the information was never in the text layer. Dropping images loses the data entirely.

Q66S10

Retrieval comes back with nothing relevant. What should a production bot do?

Answer: Refuse, and say it is not in the sources

Why: Refuse and say so. A grounded bot that cannot find support should decline rather than fill the gap from memory.

Trap: Answering from model knowledge is exactly the hallucination RAG was built to stop. Returning the nearest chunk anyway is how wrong policy reaches a customer.

Q67S10

What problem does MCP solve for agents?

Answer: One standard way to expose tools to any client

Why: Before MCP, every tool needed bespoke glue per framework. MCP is one protocol, so a server you write once works with any compliant client.

Trap: It does nothing to context size or retrieval. Think of it as a standard plug, which is the analogy used in the session.

Q68S11

When is a graph worth the complexity over a straight chain?

Answer: When you need branching, retries, cycles or a pause

Why: When control flow is the product: branching, retries, cycles, or pausing for a human. A single prompt with no tools does not need one.

Trap: Prompt length and provider count have nothing to do with it. Calling one tool does not justify a graph either.

Q69S11

What does a checkpointer give a LangGraph application?

Answer: Saved state, so a run resumes after a pause

Why: It persists graph state after each step, which is what makes pause and resume, conversational memory and crash recovery possible.

Trap: It does not speed anything up or add retrieval. It adds durability, which is what lets a run survive a restart.

Q70S11

You resume an interrupted graph under a new thread_id. What happens?

Answer: The paused state is not found, it starts fresh

Why: thread_id is the key the checkpoint is stored under. A new id points at no saved state, so the run begins from scratch and the pause context is gone.

Trap: Checkpoints never merge across threads. This is the most common human-in-the-loop bug in practice.

Q71S11

What problem does the supervisor pattern solve?

Answer: One agent with too many tools picks wrong

Why: A single agent holding twenty tools picks the wrong one. A supervisor routes to narrow specialists that each keep a small, clear toolset.

Trap: It is about routing and tool confusion, not streaming or context limits.

Q72S11

What is the loop in the ReAct pattern?

Answer: Thought, then Action, then Observation, repeat

Why: Thought, Action, Observation, then around again. The model reasons, calls a tool, reads the result, and decides what to do next.

Trap: The observation step is the part people drop. Without feeding the tool result back in, the model is guessing rather than reacting.

Q73S11

How does Tree of Thoughts differ from plain chain-of-thought?

Answer: It explores several branches and compares them

Why: Chain-of-thought follows one line of reasoning. Tree of Thoughts branches into several candidate paths, evaluates them and keeps the strongest.

Trap: It costs more tokens, not fewer. You spend that budget to escape a single bad opening step.

Q74S11

Your agent answers wrongly after six tool calls. What does tracing give you?

Answer: Every step and payload, so you see where it broke

Why: A full record of every step: prompts, tool arguments, returned values and timings, so you can see exactly which call went wrong.

Trap: Tracing is observability, not repair. It tells you where to look, then you fix the prompt, the tool or the routing yourself.

Q75S12

In an agent harness, what separates a tool from a skill?

Answer: A tool runs code, a skill is guidance it reads

Why: A tool is callable code with arguments and a return value. A skill is procedural guidance the agent loads and reads, usually markdown.

Trap: If you reversed them: tools execute, skills steer. An agent ignoring a standing rule is a skill-following failure, not a tool bug.

Q76S12

Why give an agent a file system instead of only a chat history?

Answer: Work persists and survives the context window

Why: Files let work persist beyond the context window and be revisited, so long tasks survive compaction and the agent has somewhere to think.

Trap: It has nothing to do with speed, and chat history obviously holds text. The difference is durability and structure.

Q77S12

When would you pick an agent harness over hand-writing a LangGraph?

Answer: File tools and standing rules already fit the job

Why: When file tools, standing instructions and skills already cover the workflow, so you are not writing a new node for every small behaviour change.

Trap: If you need tight explicit control of every branch, write the graph. A harness deliberately hides that control.

Q78S13

A manager asks for last week's revenue. Which path should the router take?

Answer: A read-only SQL query against the tables

Why: Revenue is a number in a table, so it comes from a read-only SQL query. Numbers must never be generated from prose or memory.

Trap: RAG over policy documents would invent a figure that sounds plausible. Keeping facts in SQL and policy in documents is the rule.

Q79S13

Why must the model never issue mutating SQL on its own?

Answer: A wrong generated write damages real data

Why: A generated UPDATE or DELETE hits real data with no review step. Writes go behind a human approval gate, which is what interrupt gives you.

Trap: Databases will happily run model-written SQL, which is precisely the danger. Speed and context length are irrelevant here.

Q80S13

A customer asks what the allergen policy says. Which source is correct?

Answer: The policy documents, retrieved and cited

Why: Policy wording lives in documents, so retrieve it and cite the source. The citation is what lets a human verify the answer.

Trap: The orders table holds transactions, not policy. Model memory is exactly what you are trying to stop it using.

Q81S13

Dining Bot parses generated SQL before running it. Why?

Answer: To prove it is SELECT-only before it runs

Why: The query is parsed and checked to be a SELECT before it reaches the database, so a generated INSERT, UPDATE or DROP is rejected rather than executed.

Trap: This is a safety gate, not a performance or translation step. Parsing beats string matching because 'delete' can hide inside a comment or a string.

Q82S13

A manager asks Dining Bot to plan next week. Why hand that to Deep Agents?

Answer: Multi-step work needs files and a plan

Why: Open-ended multi-step work needs scratch files, a plan and several passes. Deep Agents may write markdown plans, but still never writes to the database.

Trap: RAG and SQL both answer single questions well. Neither is built to produce a multi-day plan, and all three can write text.

Q83S14

Why must AWS credentials never be shipped to the browser?

Answer: Anything in client code can be extracted

Why: Anything in client code can be read by any user, including minified JavaScript and network traffic. Privilege has to stay server-side.

Trap: CloudFront does not strip secrets for you. The fix is architectural: the browser carries a token, never a credential.

Q84S14

In this stack, what does Cognito establish and what does IAM establish?

Answer: Cognito the user, IAM what the server may do

Why: Cognito authenticates the human and issues a JWT. IAM authorises your server to call the agent runtime. Two different questions.

Trap: Swapping them is the classic confusion. Identity of the user and permissions of the service are separate concerns.

Q85S14

Your React app calls the agent directly from the browser. What is wrong?

Answer: Privilege belongs on the server, not the client

Why: To call the agent you need AWS credentials, and shipping those to the client exposes them. The API must sit in between, verify the JWT, then use its IAM role.

Trap: React can certainly make the call technically. The problem is what it would have to hold in order to do so.

Q86S14

On Bedrock AgentCore, what does Gateway provide?

Answer: Tools exposed over MCP behind an auth boundary

Why: Gateway exposes tools over MCP behind an auth boundary, so your agent reaches external tools without you hand-rolling the plumbing.

Trap: Memory is recall across turns and Runtime is the managed host for the agent. Keep the three primitives distinct, interviewers ask for them by name.

Q87S14

Memory, Runtime and Identity sit in us-east-1 in these labs. Where does Gateway sit?

Answer: us-west-2, and mixing them breaks the wiring

Why: Gateway and the Cognito machine-to-machine setup are in us-west-2 in this course. Memory, Runtime and Identity stay in us-east-1.

Trap: Assuming one region is the commonest lab failure: memory ids and runtime wiring silently stop resolving. Pass the region explicitly on deploy.

Q88S14

Why define the AWS stack in CDK instead of clicking through the console?

Answer: It is reviewable, repeatable and diffable

Why: The stack is code, so it can be reviewed in a pull request, diffed before deploy and recreated identically. That is what makes the CI pipeline possible.

Trap: CDK costs the same as clicking, and the console can create everything. The difference is review and repeatability, not capability or price.

Q89S15

Your refund policy changed this morning. RAG or fine-tune?

Answer: RAG, update the document and skip training

Why: Facts that change belong in documents. Update the source and the next retrieval is correct, with no training run and no delay.

Trap: Fine-tuning to teach a changing fact means retraining every time it changes. Quantization and sampling teach nothing at all.

Q90S15

During LoRA training, what is actually being updated?

Answer: Two small matrices, the base stays frozen

Why: The base weights stay frozen. You train two small low-rank matrices whose product is added as the update.

Trap: If every weight trained you would be doing full fine-tuning, which needs roughly 112GB for a 7B model instead of a few GB.

Q91S15

For a 512x512 layer at rank 8, how many numbers does LoRA train?

Answer: 8,192, about 3% of the full 262,144

Why: B is 512x8 and A is 8x512, so 4,096 + 4,096 = 8,192, against 262,144 for the full matrix. Roughly three percent.

Trap: The formula to remember is 2 x d x r, not d squared. Bigger layers make the saving better, not worse.

Q92S15

What does the Q in QLoRA refer to?

Answer: The frozen base is quantized to 4-bit

Why: The frozen base model is quantized, typically to 4-bit NF4. The LoRA adapters on top stay at higher precision.

Trap: It is the base that gets compressed, not the adapters. That is what drops a 7B base from about 14GB to about 3.5GB.

Q93S15

You quantized a model to 4 bits. Does it now know your internal PDFs?

Answer: No, quantization compresses and teaches nothing

Why: Quantization only changes how existing weights are stored. It adds no information, so the model knows exactly what it knew before, slightly less precisely.

Trap: Compression is not learning. If you need those PDFs, that is RAG or fine-tuning data, not a bit-width change.

Q94S15

What does LoRA's alpha parameter control?

Answer: How strongly the adapter is scaled in

Why: Alpha scales the adapter's contribution, applied as alpha divided by rank. If alpha equals rank the scale is 1.

Trap: It does not decide which layers get adapters or set the learning rate. It controls how loudly the adapter speaks.

Q95S15

You need brand voice AND today's prices. What ships?

Answer: Fine-tune the voice, retrieve the prices

Why: Fine-tune the behaviour that is stable, retrieve the facts that move. Voice is stable, prices are not.

Trap: Nightly fine-tuning to chase prices is expensive and slow. And voice absolutely can be changed, which is what adapters are for.

Q96S15

What does Unsloth give you for a QLoRA run on one Colab GPU?

Answer: Faster training and less memory, same recipe

Why: Optimised kernels and memory handling, so the same LoRA or QLoRA recipe trains substantially faster and fits in less VRAM.

Trap: It is a speed and memory layer, nothing more. It does not change model size, invent data, or do anything about fresh facts, which remain RAG's job.
